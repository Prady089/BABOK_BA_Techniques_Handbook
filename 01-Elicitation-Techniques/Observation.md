# Observation (BABOK® Technique)

## 1. What This Technique Is

Observation is a technique where the Business Analyst **watches stakeholders perform their work in real time** to understand actual processes, behaviors, and interactions.  
Unlike interviews or workshops, observation focuses on **what people do**, not what they say they do.

This technique is especially valuable when work is complex, habitual, or poorly documented.

---

## 2. Why This Technique Exists

Stakeholders often:
- Omit steps they consider “obvious”
- Normalize workarounds
- Describe ideal processes instead of real ones

Observation exists to help a BA:
- Discover gaps between documented and actual processes
- Identify inefficiencies and bottlenecks
- Understand environmental and system constraints
- Capture tacit knowledge that stakeholders may not articulate

BABOK highlights observation because **real behavior often reveals root causes**.

---

## 3. When to Use Observation

Observation is most effective when:
- Processes are manual or semi-automated
- Stakeholders struggle to explain their work
- Workarounds are suspected
- Accuracy and compliance matter
- The BA is new to the domain

Common BA scenarios:
- Process improvement initiatives
- Operational efficiency studies
- Legacy system replacement
- Compliance and audit preparation

---

## 4. When NOT to Use Observation

Observation may not be appropriate when:
- Work is highly sensitive or confidential
- Observation could disrupt operations
- Activities are infrequent or event-driven
- Stakeholders feel uncomfortable being observed

In such cases, **interviews or document analysis** may be more suitable.

---

## 5. Types of Observation

### Passive Observation
The BA observes silently without interacting with the stakeholder.

### Active Observation
The BA asks clarifying questions during or after the task.

### Shadowing
The BA follows a stakeholder throughout their workday to understand end-to-end activities.

---

## 6. How to Apply Observation – Step by Step

### Step 1: Define the Objective
Clarify:
- What process or activity is being observed
- What insights are needed

### Step 2: Select the Right Participants
Choose stakeholders who:
- Perform the work regularly
- Represent typical scenarios

### Step 3: Prepare Observation Focus Areas
Identify:
- Key steps
- Decision points
- Tools and systems used
- Inputs and outputs

### Step 4: Observe the Work
- Watch without judgment
- Note deviations and workarounds
- Capture timing and hand-offs

### Step 5: Ask Clarifying Questions
Ask after observation:
- “Why was this step done?”
- “What happens if this fails?”

### Step 6: Document Findings
- Actual process steps
- Gaps vs documented process
- Pain points and risks

### Step 7: Validate Understanding
Review findings with stakeholders to confirm accuracy.

---

## 7. Inputs

Typical inputs include:
- Existing process documentation
- Problem statements
- Stakeholder list
- Compliance or audit findings

---

## 8. Outputs

Typical outputs include:
- As-is process models
- Identified inefficiencies
- Undocumented workarounds
- Improvement opportunities
- Inputs to requirements or redesign

---

## 9. Common Mistakes by Business Analysts

- Observing without a clear objective
- Interrupting work excessively
- Drawing conclusions without validation
- Ignoring environmental constraints
- Treating one observation as representative of all cases

---

## 10. Real-World Example (Banking / IT)

**Scenario:** Payment reconciliation delays

- Documented process shows automation
- Observation reveals manual Excel-based corrections
- Staff rely on tribal knowledge to resolve breaks

Outcome:
- Root causes identified
- Automation gaps exposed
- Requirements refined based on real workflows

Without observation, these issues remain hidden.

## 14. Key Takeaways

- Observation reveals reality, not assumptions
- Best for uncovering hidden workarounds
- Requires trust and discretion
- Validation is essential before drawing conclusions
